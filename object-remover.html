How It Works

Two Modes: Private & Free, or Enhanced with AI

Free mode uses inpainting — the same technique behind "content-aware fill" — to reconstruct the area you paint over using the surrounding pixels. It runs entirely on your device via your browser's WebAssembly engine, so your photo is never uploaded anywhere. It works well for logos, watermarks and objects on fairly simple backgrounds, but can look hazy or smeared on complex or highly structured backgrounds (horizons, faces, busy scenes). Enhanced (AI) mode sends the photo to Clipdrop's AI cleanup service using your own API key for noticeably better results on those tougher cases — this is not private (the image leaves your device) and uses your own Clipdrop credits.

FAQ

Common Questions

Is it legal to remove a watermark?

It depends entirely on whose image it is and what rights you have. Removing a watermark from your own photo, or one you have written permission to edit, is fine. Removing a copyright or stock-photo watermark from an image you don't own or haven't licensed is copyright infringement in most jurisdictions — this tool is intended for the former, not the latter.

Why does the result look slightly blurred, hazy or smudged?

That's a Free-mode limitation — inpainting reconstructs the painted area by blending nearby pixels, which works best on simple or repetitive backgrounds. Complex or structured backgrounds (horizons, faces, intricate patterns) are harder to reconstruct convincingly and can come out hazy. Try a tighter paint area, smaller multi-pass touch-ups, or switch to Enhanced (AI) mode for a much cleaner result on these cases.

Does this use AI / send my photo anywhere?

Only if you switch on Enhanced (AI) mode. Free mode uses classic computer-vision inpainting (OpenCV's Telea algorithm) running fully client-side via WebAssembly — your photo never leaves your device. Enhanced mode sends the photo to Clipdrop's AI cleanup API using your own API key, for better results on tougher backgrounds.

What is Enhanced (AI) mode and do I need an account?

It's an optional higher-quality removal mode powered by Clipdrop's Cleanup API. You'll need your own free Clipdrop API key (get one at clipdrop.co/apis — they offer free trial credits, then pay-as-you-go pricing). Your key is only stored in your own browser (and only if you tick "Remember on this device") — it's never sent to or stored on this site, only to Clipdrop directly.

Why did my image get resized?

Very large photos are automatically capped at 1800px on the longest side before editing, to keep processing fast and avoid crashing your browser tab. The downloaded result matches this resized dimension.

Object Removal Explained

What Is Inpainting, and How Does It Remove Objects Without Touching the Rest of the Photo?

Inpainting is a computer-vision technique that reconstructs a masked region of an image by analysing the texture, colour and structure of the pixels surrounding it. Rather than cropping, blurring or covering the area, the algorithm essentially asks "what would likely be here based on everything around it?" and fills the gap accordingly — which is exactly how professional photo editors' "content-aware fill" and "spot healing" tools work under the hood.

This tool uses the Telea inpainting algorithm from OpenCV, running entirely inside your browser via WebAssembly — the same underlying library used in countless professional computer-vision applications. You paint a mask over the region you want gone, and the algorithm works outward from the edges of that mask, propagating nearby colour and texture information inward until the area is filled. Because only the masked pixels are touched, every other pixel in your photo remains byte-for-byte identical to the original.

Results are best on simple, repetitive or low-detail backgrounds — skies, walls, grass, water, fabric. Highly detailed or structured backgrounds (faces, text, fine patterns) are inherently harder for any inpainting algorithm to reconstruct convincingly, so for tricky spots it often helps to remove smaller sections at a time using the "Touch Up Further" option, rather than painting one large area in a single pass.
